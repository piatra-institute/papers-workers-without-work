## Question

Humans domesticated the rock dove and enrolled it, over several millennia, as meat, as a manure engine, as a message-carrying network, as military signals equipment, as a laboratory model, as a racing athlete and a status object, and as a religious sign. Those enrolments ended at different times and for different reasons, and the bird stayed. Why did the withdrawal of every one of those institutional functions produce, in the twentieth-century city, a pest rather than an absence?

## Claim

Pest status here is the terminal state of an institutional process, and the process has conditions that can be stated and checked against cases.

1. **Distributed decommissioning.** No single abandonment event occurred. Functions were withdrawn asynchronously by unrelated institutions (armies, news agencies, agricultural estates, households, municipalities, clubs), each of which ended its own relation to the bird without any of them ending the population. The withdrawal was distributed in time, across institutions, and across space, which is why no actor ever had to declare the birds feral, and why no actor inherited them.

2. **The collectability condition (the paper's central result).** What a decommissioned working-animal population becomes is set by two institutional variables rather than by the animal's traits: whether the population remains *enumerable* (each individual still attached to an owner, and so reachable by a decision) and whether any institution retains a *standing obligation* to it. The four combinations are each historically instantiated: retirement (military working dogs under Robby's Law, research chimpanzees under the CHIMP Act), liquidation (the urban horse, sold down the market and rendered), managed commons (the municipal dovecote), and pestification (the feral pigeon). The urban horse was decommissioned faster and more completely than the pigeon and left no feral population, because the horse remained property to the end and had a carcass worth money. The pigeon was unreachable by any decision and worth nothing dead.

3. **The inversion of the capacities is a documentable textual fact.** The same capacities are described approvingly in the standard husbandry literature and as control problems in the standard wildlife-damage-management literature: site fidelity, tolerance of human proximity, flocking, fecundity, ledge nesting, droppings. The pest-control literature's own explanation for why removal fails is homing and site tenacity, which is the trait the fancier selected for. The inversion is in the institution's relation to the bird, and the two literatures can be laid side by side to show it.

4. **The pest category is a relation of three terms**, recognized utility, ownership, and spatial authorization, and restoring any one of them dissolves the classification without altering the animal. Same species, same city: a banded bird in a loft is an athlete, a bird in a temple courtyard is a sacred presence, a bird in a laboratory is a model organism, a bird on a ventilation unit is a pest. The managed municipal dovecote is the natural experiment, since it restores all three deliberately.

5. **Feral nonspace.** Augé's non-place is a relation to a site, so it is species-relative: the concourse a commuter passes through is a lifelong territory for a bird that nests above it. Pigeons were components of infrastructure and are now inhabitants of it, which inverts Star's rule that infrastructure becomes visible on breakdown; these birds became visible when they stopped working. Anti-roosting spikes, nets, and sloped ledges are the continuous expenditure that maintains the claim that the building was never habitat.

The closing generalization is about post-labour populations in general, stated structurally and without allegory: a technical system can retire a function in a decade, and the population that function produced outlasts it. Usefulness ends before membership does. The paper never names the contemporary case, and it must not; the pigeon argument has to be complete on its own terms.

## Cornerstone literature

Names that cannot be missing.

- **Pigeon sociology**: Colin Jerolmack (2007, 2008, 2009, 2013). The 2008 *Social Problems* article is the origin of the pest-construction argument for this species; a paper that fails to advance beyond it has no reason to exist.
- **Classification and pollution**: Mary Douglas (1966); Michael Thompson (1979) on the transient/rubbish/durable transitions; Kelsi Nagy and Phillip David Johnson II (2013); Dawn Biehler (2013) on how housing and public-health regimes manufacture pests.
- **Domestication and ferality**: Hernández-Alonso et al. (2023); Stringham et al. (2012) on the domestic ancestry of feral flocks; Larson and Fuller (2014) on the commensal pathway; Gering et al. (2019) on feralization as something other than reversion; Johnston and Janiga (1995) on urban feral ecology.
- **Animal labour**: Kendra Coulter (2016); Jason Hribal (2003); Blattner, Coulter and Kymlicka (2020) on what is owed when the work ends.
- **The comparative case**: Ann Norton Greene (2008) and McShane and Tarr (2007) on the urban horse, which is the control condition for the collectability claim.
- **Infrastructure**: Susan Leigh Star (1999); Maan Barua (2021) on non-human life as infrastructure; Marc Augé (1995) on non-places.
- **Animal political theory**: Donaldson and Kymlicka (2011) on liminal animals and denizenship, which is where the obligation question has to be answered.
- **Obsolescence**: David Edgerton (2007) on use-centred technological history, against the replacement narrative.

Primary material to reach for rather than cite second-hand: Darwin (1868) on pigeon breeds; Skinner (1960) on Project Pigeon; Levi (1941) as the standard husbandry reference; the wildlife-damage-management handbook entry on pigeons; the Tepper et al. (2018) dovecote excavation; the Senar et al. (2017) Barcelona feeding experiment.
