## Findings

### Domestication, ferality, and the genetic record

- [T2] Pigeons entered by the **commensal pathway**, the one that begins without an intention to domesticate; the framework is Zeder's, applied by Larson and Fuller, who place *Columba livia* under the commensal heading and name some pigeon populations as obligate synanthropes — Larson and Fuller (2014), *Annu. Rev. Ecol. Evol. Syst.* 45:115–136, §4.1: "Pigeons moved from rocky cliff habitats into permanent towns in the Near East." Supports §2's opening and the claim that the city is the only environment the bird was ever fitted for.
- [T1] **At least one Levantine domestication event** gave rise to all domestic breeds analysed; multiple events not excluded; more than 350 recognized breeds; the main threat to wild rock doves is replacement by ferals — Hernández-Alonso et al. (2023), *MBE* 40(11):msad243 (65 historical genomes). Supports §3.
- [T1] **Racing breeds have made substantial contributions to feral populations** — Stringham et al. (2012), *Current Biology* 22(4):302–308 (361 individuals, 70 breeds). Corroborated twice: Shapiro et al. (2013) *Science* on racing-breed contributions to North American ferals, and Gilbert and Shapiro (2014), who report feral and racing pigeons as "only minimally genetically differentiated." This is the load-bearing claim for ferality-as-omission in §3.
- [T3] Homing let domestics fly free by day and return, "thus providing plenty of opportunity for birds to go feral"; racing birds that fail to return "likely add to the feral pigeon population and gene pool"; truly wild rock pigeons "might be on the verge of genotypic extinction" — Gilbert and Shapiro (2014), *Encyclopedia of Global Archaeology*, pp. 5944–5948.
- [T1] **Feralization is not reversal.** "Feralization is not a mere reversal of domestication"; "domestication-related changes are not predictably reversed"; retained domestication alleles persist and can be adaptive; apparent reversion often uses novel genetic routes — Gering et al. (2019), *TREE* 34(12):1137–1151. Supports the §3 claim that a feral pigeon is a domestic animal minus an owner.
- [T1] **Management at Hala Sultan Tekke, Cyprus, by c. 1400 BCE**: 91% of pigeon specimens from Late Cypriot IIA–C; juveniles show breeding inside the settlement; isotopes indicate human-supplemented grain; burning patterns and element distribution show the birds were eaten, including in ritual feasting; authors characterize the birds as semi-domesticated and push direct evidence of management back roughly 1,000 years — Carter et al. (2026), *Antiquity*, First View, pp. 1–17.
- [NOT USED] Blasco et al. (2014), "The earliest pigeon fanciers," *Sci. Rep.* 4:5971, documents Neanderthal **hunting** of wild rock doves at Gorham's Cave, not domestication. The title is a trap.
- [NOT USED] No broods-per-year figure and no cliff-analogue nesting claim could be attributed to Johnston and Janiga (1995) from the primary text; the standard citations for the latter are Goodwin and Haag-Wackernagel. The paper therefore makes the cliff claim through Larson and Fuller's verified sentence instead.

### What the bird was for

- [T1] **Sa'adon dovecotes, Negev, Byzantine (5th–6th c. CE).** Circular structure 6.2 m across; triangular nesting cavities 30 cm on a side and 30 deep, in rows 80 cm above the floor; "the circular dovecote with its 1100 such cavities could have housed some 550 pairs of pigeons at any one time," producing "up to 6000–7000 hatchlings per year" and "as much as 10–11 m³ of manure per year." Square structure: "no more than 600 compartments when intact, and housed about 300 pairs," about 3,000 chicks and 5–6 m³ of manure. Structure B's floor is a pigeon dung layer with articulated skeletons; Structure A was swept, "presumably removed in an orderly fashion prior to its abandonment." Three AMS dates on pigeon bone give a combined calibrated range 530–580 CE. Mishnaic prescription: "500 liter per 1.24 acre of pigeon manure for farmed land per year." — Tepper et al. (2018), *Levant* 50(1):91–113 (12 authors).
- [T2] **Isfahan pigeon towers.** Standard pigeon-hole 20 × 20 cm by 27 deep; Chahār Burj "said to hold 10,000 birds, and provided the chief income of the village"; seventeenth-century travellers reported "over 3000 of these towers"; roughly fifty still in use in the Gavart area in the mid-1960s; "the function of the towers is the collection of manure"; towers entered once a year; towers and birds belonged to the landlord, "who paid a tax to the Shāh on the manure sold" — Beazley (1966), *Iran* 4:105–109.
- [NOT USED] The circulating "14,000 birds per tower" figure is untraceable to any academic source. Beazley also records the saltpetre claim as resting on a single seventeenth-century traveller and notes that "no other source suggests it."
- [T2] **Reuter's Aachen–Brussels pigeon service**, 1850–1851, bridged the gap between the Prussian and Belgian telegraph networks and ended when the wire closed the gap — Read (1992), *The Power of News*. The commonly repeated "45 birds / 76 miles / two hours" details trace to a Reuters-alumni site and to Wikipedia-derived pages; Read could not be opened to confirm them, so the paper states only the dated opening and closing.
- [T1/T2] **US Army pigeon service, 1917–1957.** Authorized November 1917; in the Second World War the Signal Corps furnished about 54,000 birds, roughly 40,000 of them donated by civilian fanciers; at disbandment 15 surviving hero pigeons went to zoos and about 1,000 birds were sold to the public; the last bird was auctioned 23 March 1957 — US Army (army.mil and CECOM Historian), corroborated across three official pages. This is the paper's enumerable-decommissioning case in §3 and §4.
- [T1] **Cher Ami is an institutional artefact.** "There is nothing conclusive linking the pigeon to the actions of the Lost Battalion... The U.S. Army chose to link Cher Ami with the Lost Battalion's story to promote the contributions of the Signal Corps' Pigeon Service" — Blazich (2021), *Journal of Military History* 85(3):646–677. DNA sampling in 2021 determined the bird was male after a century of contradictory records.
- [T1] **Dickin Medal**: 75 awards since 1943, of which 38 dogs, 32 pigeons, 4 horses, 1 cat — PDSA, accessed 2026-07-29. The often-quoted "32 of 54 wartime medals" split is secondary; the cumulative figures are the organization's own.
- [T2] **Operation Columba**: more than 16,000 pigeons dropped into occupied Europe April 1941 – September 1944, about one in ten returning, roughly 1,000 carrying messages; run by MI14(d) — Corera (2018), *Secret Pigeon Service* (UK title; the US edition is *Operation Columba*).
- [T1] **Darwin's reason for the pigeon chapters**: "In one case alone, namely in that of the domestic pigeon, I will describe fully all the chief races"; the argument turns on the taxonomist's eye, "at least a score of pigeons might be chosen, which if shown to an ornithologist, and he were told that they were wild birds, would certainly, I think, be ranked by him as well-defined species," and "I can feel no doubt that all our domestic breeds have descended from the *Columba livia*." — Darwin (1868), *Variation* vol. I, ch. V pp. 131–179 and ch. VI pp. 180–223; *Origin* (1859) ch. I.
- [T1] **Project Pigeon.** "This is the history of a crackpot idea"; the committee demonstration "was a perfect performance, but it had just the wrong effect"; "The basic difficulty, of course, lay in convincing a dozen distinguished physical scientists that the behavior of a pigeon could be adequately controlled"; OSRD refused to extend the contract in January 1944 on the ground that other projects "would have more immediate promise of combat application"; **"The equipment was scrapped, but 30 of the pigeons were kept to see how long they would retain the appropriate behavior"**; "even after six years of inactivity a pigeon will immediately and correctly strike a target to which it has been conditioned" — Skinner (1960), *American Psychologist* 15(1):28–37. This is the paper's opening and closing figure.
- [T1] **Rooftop flyers.** Pigeon flying "fosters solidarity among Italian, Hispanic and African American New Yorkers of varying ages," against a community-studies literature that predicts conflict; status is contested at pet-shop gatherings on the basis of the birds' performance — Jerolmack (2009), *Ethnography* 10(4):435–457. Note the abstract never uses the word masculinity; the paper does not attribute it.
- [NOT USED] Levi (1941), *The Pigeon*, is the standard husbandry reference (published by the author, Sumter SC; 2nd rev. ed. 1957), but no copy could be opened. The trait-inversion argument therefore rests entirely on the control literature quoting itself, which is stronger evidence anyway.

### The pest category

- [T1] **Jerolmack's corpus and dates.** *New York Times* 1851–2006 (498 articles found, 85 plus 12 op-eds analysed) and 51 other papers 1980–2006 (162 titled articles from 458). 1935: the first problematization apart from a specific incident, a letter saying pigeons outside parks and squares "are entirely out of place." 1945: first article mentioning a specific disease. 1 October 1963: a city health official ascribes two deaths to pigeon-borne disease and calls for a campaign against the city's 5,000,000 pigeons, "the first deaths directly blamed on pigeons, and also the first time an estimate on the number of pigeons was published." 22 June 1966: Thomas Hoving, parks commissioner, calls the pigeon "a rat with wings" in the *NYT*; Mayor Koch quotes it approvingly in 1979; *Stardust Memories* (1980) is "often erroneously considered to be the origin of the phrase." — Jerolmack (2008), *Social Problems* 55(1):72–94.
- [T1/T2] **"Matter out of place" predates Douglas and she says so** ("the *old* definition of dirt"). Earliest documented instance: Palmerston to the Royal Agricultural Society, *The Times*, 16 July 1852, p. 8: "I have heard it said that dirt is nothing but a thing in a wrong place. Now, the dirt of our towns precisely corresponds with that definition." The context was a debate about urban waste as agricultural fertilizer. Douglas later attributed the phrase to Chesterfield without a source; neither her biographer nor Macfarlane could find it there — Campkin (2013), *Architectural Theory Review* 18(1):46–61; Fardon (2013), *Anthropology Today* 29(1):25–27. This is the §6 rhyme with the dovecote.
- [T1] **Health risk, measured.** 176 documented transmissions of illness from feral pigeons to humans between 1941 and 2003; 60 human-pathogenic organisms harboured, of which 7 were actually transmitted; 99.4% of incidents were aerosol transmissions; risk very low except for the immunocompromised — Haag-Wackernagel and Moch (2004), *Journal of Infection* 48(4):307–313.
- [T1] **NYC Department of Health**, "Pigeon-Related Diseases": "The risk of pigeon-related diseases is rare. People most at risk from these diseases are those who have a compromised immune system." "A routine cleaning of droppings, such as from a windowsill, does not pose a serious health risk to most people." "It is not illegal to feed or keep pigeons."
- [T1] **Barcelona feeding experiment.** Two experimental districts and one control neighbourhood, 44 experimental and 12 control quadrats, education campaign 1 Feb 2009 – 22 Feb 2010, 2,190 citizens contacted; "The number of pigeons at the two experimental districts was reduced by 40% between February and June," the control district did not vary, and "the reduction was mainly related to the reduction in food availability but not to culling," although culling removed 5,935 + 4,083 + 2,252 = 12,270 birds — Senar et al. (2017), *Pest Management Science* 73(2):313–317.
- [T2] **Venice.** "It was estimated that pigeons were fed 350 tons of corn per year and the number of pigeons present in St Mark's Square was critically high, reaching concentrations of >10,000 individuals in 1.3 ha. In May 2008, the local Authorities decided to ban the distribution of corn and since then the number of pigeons has decreased dramatically, down to a maximum of 1000 individuals at one time" — Giunchi et al. (2012), in *Integrated Pest Management and Pest Control*, pp. 215–240.
- [T1] **The billion-dollar figure is an extrapolation.** "The control costs for pigeons are at least $9 per pigeon per year... Assuming that there is one pigeon per hectare in urban areas..., or approximately 0.5 pigeons per person, and using potential control costs as a surrogate for losses, pigeons cause $1.1 billion per year in damages" — Pimentel et al. (2000), *BioScience* 50(1):53–65, p. 56; reprinted unrevised in Pimentel et al. (2005). Methodological critique of the programme: Boltovskoy et al. (2022), *Ambio* 51(8):1786–1799.
- [T2] **Rubbish theory's transitions.** Transient, rubbish, durable; transient → rubbish and rubbish → durable are permitted, the reverse directions blocked, and rubbish is a covert category — Thompson (1979), via Bernard Williams's *LRB* review (1980) for the wording of the blocked transitions. The paper does not quote Thompson directly.
- [T1] **"Pestification" is taken.** Coined by Saleh et al. (2014), *New Phytologist* 201(4):1440–1456, for the influence of rice domestication on *Magnaporthe oryzae*; defined by De Gracia et al. (2015), *PLoS ONE* 10(6):e0122909, as "the process by which a pathogen originally living in the wild become adapted to biotic and abiotic crop conditions." The paper acknowledges the prior sense and distinguishes its own as the administrative counterpart.
- [T1] **"Distributed decommissioning" is available.** Phrase searches return only nuclear, offshore and IT-asset engineering uses; no term-of-art usage in animal studies. Likewise "decommissioned animal."
- [T1] **The control literature convicts itself.** From Williams and Corrigan (1994), pp. E-87–E-96: "Pigeons are highly dependent on humans to provide them with food and sites for roosting, loafing, and nesting"; "The common pigeon was introduced into the United States as a domesticated bird, but many escaped and formed feral populations. The pigeon is now the most common bird pest associated with people"; "Pigeons inhabit lofts, steeples, attics, caves, and ornate architectural features"; "in some urban areas the feeding of pigeons is considered a form of recreation"; "More eggs are laid before the first clutch leaves the nest"; natural mortality "reduce[s] pigeon populations by approximately 30% annually"; "Feral pigeons are not protected by federal law and most states do not afford them protection"; **"Releasing pigeons back to the 'wild' is impractical. Pigeons are likely to return even when released 50 or more miles (>80 km) from the problem site, or become pests in other communities"**; disposal by CO/CO2 chamber built from "a garbage can or 55 gallon (209 l) drum," birds expiring "in 5 to 7 minutes"; "Roosting on ledges can be discouraged by changing the angle to 45° or more"; Avitrol works because "the dying birds exhibit distress behavior that frightens other members of the flock away," diluted "to minimize the mortality and maximize the flock-alarming reactions."

### The comparative case: the urban horse

- [T1] **Populations.** Manhattan 130,000 horses in 1900, Chicago 74,000, Philadelphia 51,000 (p. 17); large nineteenth-century cities averaged about one horse per 20 people; the 1900 urban horse population was about 3 million, consuming 7.7 million tons of hay and 8.8 million tons of oats (pp. 33–34) — McShane and Tarr (2007).
- [T1] **Manure.** 30–50 pounds per day per heavy urban animal, "probably averaging around seven tons a year" (p. 26); the market was destroyed by South Carolina rock phosphates (p. 27) — McShane and Tarr (2007). The parallel with the dovecote economy is the paper's, not theirs.
- [T1] **The exit.** "The urban horse commonly had a career that began with a training period doing light farm work... then he hauled for a street railway firm, which sold him to a delivery firm, which sold him to a cab firm, which sold him to a peddler... **Lameness and then the veterinarian's pistol was the most common end for city horses**" (p. 25). "Shooting lame horses was commonplace, just as one might junk a worn-out engine. And the dead horse would frequently be recycled at an animal rendering plant, the organic equivalent of a scrap iron processor" (p. 6). Street railways depreciated horses over five years and sold them even if healthy (p. 6). Between 1887 and 1897 New York's ASPCA shot between 1,800 and 7,000 horses a year, "the beginning of the familiar paradox by which humane societies have become the leading killers of animals in cities" (p. 27). In 1910, "probably the peak year," New York removed more than 20,595 dead horses from its streets (p. 28). The New York Rendering Company held a monopoly and guaranteed removal within three hours, because "after rigor mortis set in, hides lost their value" (p. 28). One St. Louis rendering firm in 1896 claimed a 24-dollar profit per carcass (p. 29). — McShane and Tarr (2007). This is the whole of §4's collectability argument.
- [T2] **National series.** US equine population 1900 = 21.5 million, 1915 = 26,493,000 (peak in this series), 1950 = 7,604,000, 1960 = 3,089,000 — Ensminger via Kilby (2007), *The State of the Animals 2007*, Table 1. Caveat recorded: USDA's own annual farm series peaks later, 1918–1919 at about 26.4 million, so the paper says "peaked in the 1910s" rather than naming a year.

### The statutes, read directly

- [T1] **Wild Free-Roaming Horses and Burros Act**, Public Law 92-195, approved **15 December 1971** (not 18 December, which circulates widely), 85 Stat. 649. §1: "wild free-roaming horses and burros are living symbols of the historic and pioneer spirit of the West... shall be protected from capture, branding, harassment, or death; and to accomplish this they are to be considered in the area where presently found, as an integral part of the natural system of the public lands." §2(b) defines the class as **"all unbranded and unclaimed horses and burros on public lands of the United States."** The statutory definition is the paper's non-enumerable cell, written by Congress.
- [T1] **Robby's Law**, Public Law 106-446, 6 November 2000, 114 Stat. 1932, codified at 10 U.S.C. §2582, titled "Military working dogs: transfer and adoption at **end of useful working life**." Before it, euthanasia was the standard disposition.
- [T1] **CHIMP Act**, Public Law 106-551, 20 December 2000, 114 Stat. 2752: a sanctuary system for chimpanzees "designated as being no longer needed in research," obliged to provide "lifetime care," with the prohibition "that the chimpanzees may not be discharged from the system."
- [T1] **BLM wild horse and burro statistics**, 1 March 2026: on-range 85,466 (61,523 horses, 23,943 burros) against a national high appropriate management level of 25,592; a large off-range holding population in corrals and pastures.
- [T1] **National Research Council (2013)** found the programme "has not used scientifically rigorous methods to estimate the population sizes of horses and burros, to model the effects of management actions on the animals, or to assess the availability and use of forage."

### Space, infrastructure, and the spike

- [T1] **Augé's definition** (1995 Verso ed., pp. 77–79): "If a place can be defined as relational, historical and concerned with identity, then a space which cannot be defined as relational, or historical, or concerned with identity will be a non-place." Also: place and non-place "are rather like opposed polarities... like palimpsests"; the non-place "never exists in pure form." The paper's species-relativity move extends a concept that is already relational; it does not claim Augé made it observer-relative.
- [T1] **Star's nine properties** (1999, pp. 381–382), including transparency, learned as part of membership, installed base, and "becomes visible upon breakdown"; infrastructure "means different things to different groups"; the staircase is infrastructure for the walker and a barrier for the wheelchair user. The paper's inversion (visible upon **decommissioning**) is stated against this.
- [T1] **Barua (2021)**: three modes, infrastructural circulation, infrastructure as a medium of non-human inhabitation through repurposing against design intent, and non-human life cast as infrastructure. Both ends of the paper's double-infrastructure claim are his.
- [T1] **Philo and Wilbert (2000)**: "animal spaces" (human orderings) versus "beastly places" (animals' own geographies); animals "transgressing, even resisting, human spatial orderings." The phrase "matter out of place" is **not** verified in their introduction and is not attributed to them.
- [T1] **Haque (2026)**, "interstitial ecologies," Karachi road margins and flyover undersides; **Juola (2024)**, a pigeon colony in an abandoned pulp-mill silo at Meri-Toppila, Oulu, birds thriving "in places that we no longer consider valuable."
- [T1] **Spike chronology.** US Patent 2,142,371 "Birdproofing" (Peles), filed 10 June 1938, granted 3 January 1939; US Patent 2,777,171 "Bird and Rodent Barrier" (Burnside, Plath and Kaufmann), filed 19 January 1951, the Nixalite porcupine-wire design, commercially sold from 1950. The London anti-homeless spikes controversy broke in June 2014. Chronological priority of the bird spike is documentable; no scholarly source calls it the design ancestor, so the paper claims only the chronology.
- [T1] **Petty (2016)**, *International Journal for Crime, Justice and Social Democracy* 5(1):67–81 (**not** *IJLBE* 8(1), a locator that circulates wrongly): objectors compared the spikes "to mechanisms used in London to deter pigeons from landing on the ledges of buildings," and a resident said "it's like they are treating people like animals" (p. 75). Petty records the public analogy; he does not theorize it.
- [T1] **Donaldson and Kymlicka (2011)**, ch. 7: pigeons are named among liminal animals; denizenship comprises secure residency, fair terms of reciprocity (shared space and reproduction may be regulated by non-rights-violating means), and anti-stigma safeguards against vilification as pests or vermin.
- [T1] **Cochrane (2019)**, pp. 48–64: membership recognition carries "rights to residency, healthcare, retirement provision, political representation, and more," and the animals who have received anything of the kind are police and military animals holding "quasi-membership status." Used in preference to the Donaldson and Kymlicka chapter in the same volume, which could not be opened.
- [T2] **The honest report on dovecotes.** On egg replacement in urban dovecotes: "we are not aware of any quantitative estimation of the efficacy of this kind of approach for pest control." On culling: "no scientific study has demonstrated the efficacy of this approach in significantly affecting population size." — Giunchi et al. (2012).
- [T1] **The fancy is counting itself out.** The Royal Pigeon Racing Association's own site estimates 60,000 members in 1989; its chief executive's report of November 2024 gives just under 14,000. Belgian and American figures were found but not at a citable tier and are excluded.
- [NOT USED] Basel's "20,000 down to 10,000" is untraceable to the primary paper (Haag-Wackernagel 1995, *Wildlife Society Bulletin* 23(2):256–260, which could not be opened), so the paper uses Barcelona and Venice instead. Trafalgar Square's pre-ban counts are journalistic estimates; the Greater London Authority holds no statistical data from before the byelaws. The Wikipedia figure of 35,000 birds at peak is inconsistent with every other source.
